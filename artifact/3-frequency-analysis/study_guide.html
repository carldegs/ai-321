1. Harmonics are coordinates, not separate signals

A periodic continuous-time waveform satisfies \(x(t+T)=x(t)\), with time \(t\), period \(T>0\), fundamental angular frequency \(\omega_0=2\pi/T\) in rad/s, and ordinary frequency \(f_0=1/T\) in Hz. Its complex Fourier series is a coordinate expansion in orthogonal harmonics: \[x(t)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}C_ke^{jk\omega_0t},\qquad C_k=\frac1T\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}x(t)e^{-jk\omega_0t}\,dt.\] Here \(j^2=-1\), integer \(k\) labels the harmonic, \(t_0\) is any starting time, and \(C_k\) is its complex weight. Orthogonality over a complete period makes each coefficient a projection: other harmonics integrate to zero. A common period of a sum exists when frequencies are commensurate; it need not simply be the longest component period for arbitrary periodic signals.Source evidence: CT deck p3, CT deck p5, CT deck p7, CT deck p17, CT deck p18, CT deck p23.

The real-coordinate form uses \(a_0/2+\sum_{k\ge1}[a_k\cos(k\omega_0t)+b_k\sin(k\omega_0t)]\), where \(a_k,b_k\) are real coefficients. Thus \(C_0=a_0/2\), \(C_k=(a_k-jb_k)/2\), and \(C_{-k}=C_k^*\) for a real signal, with star denoting complex conjugation. The real spectral plot is even and the imaginary plot odd; negative frequencies carry the conjugate coordinate required to reconstruct a real waveform. In the square-wave spectrum, the horizontal axis is harmonic index, the DC stem is 0.5, and only odd-index imaginary stems remain, decaying as \(1/|k|\). The sawtooth retains every harmonic, while the triangular-wave examples decay as \(1/k^2\), reflecting a smoother time waveform.Source evidence: CT deck p14, CT deck p16, CT deck p22, CT deck p27, CT deck p28, CT deck p34, CT deck p36, DT deck p15, DT deck p16, DT deck p17.

Time origin changes phase rather than spectral magnitude: for a delay \(\tau\), the new coefficient is \(C_ke^{-jk\omega_0\tau}\). Even/odd symmetry is therefore meaningful relative to the chosen origin. The partial synthesis plots show sharper transitions as more harmonics enter, but discontinuities are a convergence issue rather than a missing-frequency defect. Tutorial extension: under the usual piecewise-smooth conditions the series takes the midpoint value at a jump; Gibbs overshoot becomes spatially narrower with increasing order but its relative height does not vanish. The listed Dirichlet conditions are sufficient, not necessary.Source evidence: CT deck p29, CT deck p31, CT deck p32, DT deck p13, DT deck p14, DT deck p18.

Study trace · tutorial extension

For a pulse equal to one over the first half-period and zero over the second, integrate separately for \(k=0\) to obtain \(C_0=1/2\). For nonzero odd \(k\), \(C_k=-j/(\pi k)\); even coefficients vanish. Pair positive and negative terms to recover the sine series and check the mean.Source evidence: CT deck p25, CT deck p27.

Exam focus

Never infer phase from magnitude alone. Use quadrant-aware \(\operatorname{atan2}(a_k,b_k)\) for the sine-phase convention in CT p14; a single arctangent ratio loses quadrant information. A zero coefficient has undefined phase.Source evidence: CT deck p14.

2. From line spectra to a continuous frequency density

Let a rectangular pulse have amplitude \(A\), width \(\tau\), and periodic repetition interval \(T\). Its series coefficients satisfy \[C_k=\frac{A\tau}{T}\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{k\omega_0\tau}{2}\right),\quad \operatorname{sinc}(v)=\frac{\sin v}{v},\quad \operatorname{sinc}(0)=1.\] The dimensionless argument \(v\) and the unnormalized sinc convention are explicit here. Increasing \(T\) at fixed \(\tau\) decreases both line spacing \(2\pi/T\) and individual coefficient height. The CT p40 plots put time on the left and scaled coefficients \(TC_k\) on the right: the envelope stays fixed while the frequency sampling gets denser. This scaled envelope, rather than the vanishing raw coefficients, leads to the aperiodic transform.Source evidence: CT deck p38, CT deck p39, CT deck p40, CT deck p41, CT deck p42.

The continuous-time Fourier transform (CTFT) pair is \[X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)e^{-j\omega t}\,dt,\qquad x(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}\,d\omega.\] Here \(\omega\) is continuous angular frequency and \(X\) has signal-amplitude times time units. A single pulse has \(X(j\omega)=A\tau\operatorname{sinc}(\omega\tau/2)\), with DC height \(A\tau\) and first zeros at \(\omega=\pm2\pi/\tau\). Narrowing its width lowers the DC height at fixed amplitude and broadens the main lobe; the sign-changing sidelobes are not a nonnegative magnitude spectrum. For \(x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)\), with decay rate \(a>0\) and unit step \(u\), \(X=1/(a+j\omega)\): the magnitude peaks at \(1/a\), rolls off with frequency, and the phase approaches opposite \(\pi/2\) limits.Source evidence: CT deck p43, CT deck p45, CT deck p46, CT deck p47, CT deck p48, CT deck p51, CT deck p52, CT deck p53.

Absolute integrability guarantees an ordinary CTFT, but constants, sinusoids, impulses, and steps require distributions. The relevant pairs are \(\delta(t)\leftrightarrow1\), \(1\leftrightarrow2\pi\delta(\omega)\), and \(e^{j\omega_ct}\leftrightarrow2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_c)\), where \(\delta\) is the unit-area Dirac distribution and \(\omega_c\) a carrier frequency. A delayed impulse changes phase by \(-\omega\tau\). Tutorial clarification: the step transform \(\pi\delta(\omega)+\operatorname{PV}[1/(j\omega)]\) uses the Cauchy principal value, abbreviated PV; it is not an ordinary divergent integral assigned a finite value.Source evidence: CT deck p49, CT deck p50, CT deck p54, CT deck p63, CT deck p64, CT deck p65, CT deck p66, CT deck p67, CT deck p68.

Study trace · tutorial extension

Hold pulse area \(A\tau\) fixed while shrinking \(\tau\). The amplitude must rise, and the sinc spectrum tends to a flat constant over each finite frequency range. This is the impulse limit; holding amplitude fixed instead sends the pulse area to zero.Source evidence: CT deck p48, CT deck p49.

Exam focus

Correction to CT p45: ordinary frequency is \(f=\omega/(2\pi)\), not \(2\pi\omega\). Different unitary or Hz transform conventions change normalization. CT p62 calls \(X(0)\) an average, but it is the integral of an aperiodic signal; a periodic average is \(C_0\).Source evidence: CT deck p45.

3. Sampling makes frequency periodic; time periodicity makes it discrete

For integer sample index \(n\), \(e^{j(\Omega+2\pi m)n}=e^{j\Omega n}\) for every integer \(m\). Thus discrete-time angular frequency \(\Omega\) is measured in rad/sample and is equivalent modulo \(2\pi\). A sinusoid is periodic in \(n\) only if \(\Omega_0/(2\pi)\) is rational. The DT p23 irrational-frequency cosine has no finite time period even though it retains sinusoidal spectral lines in the distributional sense. For a signal of integer period \(N\), the discrete-time Fourier series (DTFS) contains only \(N\) distinct coefficients: \[x[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}c_ke^{j2\pi kn/N},\qquad c_k=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.\] The sequence \(c_k\) repeats with period \(N\); \(k\) is a harmonic index, not a frequency in Hz.Source evidence: DT deck p21, DT deck p22, DT deck p23, DT deck p24, DT deck p25, DT deck p26.

For an aperiodic sequence, the DTFT is continuous but periodic: \[X(e^{j\Omega})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]e^{-j\Omega n},\qquad x[n]=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\Omega})e^{j\Omega n}\,d\Omega.\] Any complete interval of length \(2\pi\) gives the same inverse. The four-transform comparison therefore follows two separate rules: discrete time gives periodic frequency; periodic time gives discrete spectral lines. Tutorial extension: if physical sample spacing is \(T_s\), then \(\Omega=\omega T_s=2\pi fT_s\); frequencies differing by an integer multiple of \(1/T_s\) are indistinguishable in the samples. A DFT of a finite record can be understood as DTFS coefficients of a repeated record with normalization adjusted.Source evidence: DT deck p7, DT deck p8, DT deck p9, DT deck p10, DT deck p39, DT deck p40, DT deck p41, DT deck p42.

A length-\(L\) pulse of amplitude \(A\), supported on \(n=0,\ldots,L-1\), has \[X(e^{j\Omega})=Ae^{-j\Omega(L-1)/2}\frac{\sin(L\Omega/2)}{\sin(\Omega/2)},\qquad X(1)=AL.\] Here \(L\) is an integer duration and the apparent singularities at multiples of \(2\pi\) are removable. Repeating the pulse every \(N\) samples gives \(c_k=X(e^{j2\pi k/N})/N\). The DT p33 comparisons must distinguish duration and repetition: increasing \(L\) narrows the envelope in angular frequency, while increasing \(N\) samples that envelope more densely. In harmonic-index coordinates the first-zero location scales as \(N/L\); it cannot be interpreted without the axis conversion. The phase has a linear slope from the pulse midpoint plus \(\pi\) changes where the real sine ratio reverses sign.Source evidence: DT deck p27, DT deck p28, DT deck p29, DT deck p30, DT deck p31, DT deck p32, DT deck p33, DT deck p34, DT deck p43, DT deck p44, DT deck p45, DT deck p46.

Study trace · tutorial extension

For \(x[n]=[1,1,0,0]\) repeated with \(N=4\), obtain \(c_0=1/2\), \(c_1=(1-j)/4\), \(c_2=0\), and \(c_3=(1+j)/4\). Compare conjugate symmetry, the mean, and phase at the zero bin before plotting.Source evidence: DT deck p25.

Finite-sum boundary check (DT p30): \(\sum_{n=0}^{N}r^n=(1-r^{N+1})/(1-r)\); if the last index is \(N-1\), the numerator is \(1-r^N\). The DT p33 heading says PSD but its vertical labels are \(|c_k|\); squared magnitude is the power contribution.Source evidence: DT deck p30, DT deck p33.

4. Convolution, modulation, and energy are one transform calculus

For an LTI system with input \(x\), impulse response \(h\), and output \(y=x*h\), convolution becomes multiplication: \(Y=HX\). This permits a frequency-by-frequency interpretation of filtering, with gain \(|H|\) and phase \(\arg H\). CT differentiation corresponds to multiplication by \(j\omega\); a time delay corresponds to \(e^{-j\omega\tau}\). Multiplication of two time signals instead convolves their spectra, including a factor \(1/(2\pi)\) in this angular-frequency convention. Thus multiplying by a cosine with carrier \(\omega_c\) produces half-amplitude spectral copies shifted to \(\pm\omega_c\), rather than a single translated spectrum.Source evidence: CT deck p55, CT deck p56, CT deck p57, CT deck p58, CT deck p60, CT deck p61, CT deck p69, DT deck p50, DT deck p51, DT deck p53, DT deck p54.

Parseval separates finite energy from average power. A finite-energy DT signal satisfies \[E=\sum_n|x[n]|^2=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|X(e^{j\Omega})|^2\,d\Omega;\qquad P=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|x[n]|^2=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|c_k|^2.\] Here \(E\) is energy, \(P\) average power, and \(N\) the period in the second identity. An indefinitely repeating nonzero signal has infinite energy but finite average power. For deterministic finite-energy signals, \(S_{xx}=|X|^2\) is energy spectral density; for periodic signals, individual \(|c_k|^2\) are line powers. A stochastic power spectral density requires a stationary-process correlation definition, so these objects should not be conflated merely because each is called a spectrum.Source evidence: CT deck p59, DT deck p32, DT deck p47, DT deck p52.

For complex sequences use conjugated correlation \(r_{xx}[\ell]=\sum_nx[n]x^*[n-\ell]\), with integer lag \(\ell\). Its DTFT is \(|X|^2\). Real-signal formulas can suppress the star because conjugation has no effect there; extending them to complex data without it gives the wrong spectrum. For the real decaying sequence \(x[n]=a^nu[n]\), \(|a|<1\), \[S_{xx}(\Omega)=\frac1{1-2a\cos\Omega+a^2}.\] At \(a=0.5\), the plot has height 4 at zero and height \(4/9\) at \(\pi\): persistence concentrates energy near DC. Reversing the sign of \(a\) shifts concentration toward \(\pi\), an alternating sequence. Tutorial extension: time windowing convolves a spectrum with the window spectrum; the rectangular window sidelobes spread a narrowband component, explaining leakage.Source evidence: DT deck p48, DT deck p49, DT deck p52, DT deck p57, DT deck p58, Z deck p34.

5. A z-transform is a function together with its region of convergence

The bilateral z-transform extends the DTFT from the unit circle into the complex plane: \[X(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]z^{-n},\qquad z=re^{j\Omega}.\] Here \(r=|z|>0\) is radius and \(\Omega\) angle; \(z^{-n}=r^{-n}e^{-j\Omega n}\) weights the sequence exponentially before Fourier analysis. The region of convergence (ROC) records where the series converges. For \(x[n]=a^nu[n]\), \(X(z)=1/(1-az^{-1})\) with \(|z|>|a|\), whereas \(-a^nu[-n-1]\) has the same algebraic expression with \(|z|<|a|\). A rational expression without its ROC does not determine a unique time sequence.Source evidence: Z deck p3, Z deck p4, Z deck p5, Z deck p8, Z deck p15, Z deck p16, Z deck p17, Z deck p37.

The ROC sketches place real \(z\) horizontally and imaginary \(z\) vertically. A right-sided sequence converges outside its outermost pole, a left-sided sequence inside its innermost pole, and a genuinely two-sided sequence can produce an annulus. Poles cannot belong to the ROC. Finite sequences have no finite nonzero singularities, although positive-time support can exclude zero and negative-time support can exclude infinity. The DTFT is the evaluation \(X(e^{j\Omega})\) when the unit circle lies in the ROC; its existence is therefore tied to convergence, not just symbolic substitution.Source evidence: Z deck p6, Z deck p7, Z deck p13, Z deck p14, Z deck p15, Z deck p16, Z deck p17, Z deck p44, Z deck p47, Z deck p50.

Transform properties move both algebra and convergence. A shift by integer \(q\) gives \(x[n-q]\leftrightarrow z^{-q}X(z)\); multiplying by \(a^n\) maps \(X(z)\) to \(X(z/a)\) and scales ROC radii by \(|a|\). Reversal maps \(X(z)\) to \(X(z^{-1})\) and reciprocates annulus boundaries. Multiplication by \(n\) gives \(-z\,dX/dz\), preserving the ordinary annular ROC. Convolution multiplies transforms, and its ROC contains at least the intersection of the individual ROCs; cancellation can enlarge it. For causal sequences the initial value follows as \(x[0]=\lim_{z\to\infty}X(z)\), provided the transform exists around infinity.Source evidence: Z deck p18, Z deck p20, Z deck p24, Z deck p26, Z deck p28, Z deck p30, Z deck p32, Z deck p36, Z deck p39, Z deck p40.

Study trace · tutorial extension

Take \(X(z)=z/(z-2)\). The causal inverse grows as \(2^n\), and the ROC \(|z|>2\) excludes the unit circle. The anticausal inverse is \(-2^nu[-n-1]\); its ROC \(|z|<2\) contains the unit circle and its absolute sum is finite. Identical poles do not imply identical causality or stability.Source evidence: Z deck p37.

6. Poles connect recursions, stability, and frequency selectivity

Complex exponentials are eigenfunctions of an LTI system whenever the defining response converges. The DT introductory s-plane diagrams put decay or growth on the real axis and oscillation on the imaginary axis: \(s=\sigma+j\omega\), where \(\sigma\) is exponential growth rate and \(\omega\) angular frequency. Tutorial extension: sampling an exponential every \(T_s\) seconds maps \(s\) to \(z=e^{sT_s}\); the CT left half-plane maps inside the unit circle. Under initial rest, a constant-coefficient recursion \[y[n]+\sum_{k=1}^{P}a_ky[n-k]=\sum_{k=0}^{Q}b_kx[n-k]\] produces \(H(z)=Y(z)/X(z)=(\sum_{k=0}^{Q}b_kz^{-k})/(1+\sum_{k=1}^{P}a_kz^{-k})\). Here \(P,Q\) are feedback and feedforward orders, \(a_k,b_k\) coefficients, and \(X,Y\) input/output transforms. Feedforward terms form a finite impulse response (FIR); feedback can form an infinite impulse response (IIR). Rational factoring locates zeros where transfer vanishes and poles where it diverges, after common-factor cancellation. In a real-coefficient system, nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs. Tutorial clarification: poles and zeros locate the transfer up to gain, while causality still requires an ROC.Source evidence: DT deck p3, DT deck p4, DT deck p5, DT deck p6, Z deck p9, Z deck p10, Z deck p11, Z deck p41, Z deck p42, Z deck p43, Z deck p44, Z deck p45, Z deck p46, Z deck p48, Z deck p49.

BIBO stability means every bounded input yields bounded output, equivalently \(\sum_n|h[n]|<\infty\) for an LTI system. Its ROC must contain the unit circle. For a causal rational system this reduces to every uncanceled pole lying strictly inside \(|z|=1\). A first-order filter \(H(z)=1/(1-az^{-1})\), \(|a|<1\), has magnitude \(1/\sqrt{1-2a\cos\Omega+a^2}\): positive \(a\) emphasizes low frequencies, negative \(a\) emphasizes frequencies near \(\pi\). Conjugate poles near the unit circle create narrow resonances near their angular positions; zeros on the circle force notches. For the comb \(H=1-z^{-4}\), \(|H(e^{j\Omega})|=2|\sin(2\Omega)|\), with zeros at \(\Omega=m\pi/2\), integer \(m\); its plotted dB dips mark rejection rather than unstable poles.Source evidence: Z deck p10, Z deck p11, Z deck p12, Z deck p47, Z deck p50.

A block diagram translates delay blocks to \(z^{-1}\), branches to coefficient multiplication, and summing nodes to addition. In Z p52-59 the feedforward stage is \(H_1=1-3z^{-1}+0.4z^{-2}\) and feedback stage \(H_2=1/(1+2.5z^{-1})\), with intermediate signal \(v[n]\). Cascading multiplies these functions; exchanging stage order gives the same zero-state transfer and permits shared delays in Direct Form II. The pole at \(-2.5\) makes the causal example unstable. Tutorial extension: equal exact-arithmetic transfer does not guarantee equal numerical behavior under coefficient quantization, saturation, or nonzero internal initial conditions; a pole-zero cancellation can conceal an internally unstable mode.Source evidence: Z deck p51, Z deck p52, Z deck p53, Z deck p54, Z deck p55, Z deck p56, Z deck p57, Z deck p58, Z deck p59.

Study trace · tutorial extension

For \(H(z)=1/(1+0.81z^{-2})\), poles are \(\pm0.9j\): the causal response is stable and resonates near \(\pi/2\). By contrast \(1/(1+z^{-2})\) puts poles on the unit circle. Its natural response can oscillate indefinitely, but it is not BIBO stable.Source evidence: Z deck p11, Z deck p47, Z deck p50.

Synthesis questions

These are tutorial problems connecting the decks. Reveal each answer after deriving your own.

1. Can a magnitude spectrum distinguish a centered pulse from a delayed pulse?

No. A delay multiplies the transform by a unit-magnitude factor. Recover the pulse center from the linear phase slope, modulo phase unwrap ambiguities. The DC height and first-zero spacing recover area and width, but cannot locate the pulse in time.

2. A sampled sinusoid has angular frequency 7π/3 rad/sample. Find its equivalent baseband frequency and shortest period.

Equivalent frequency is \(\pi/3\), obtained by subtracting \(2\pi\). Its normalized frequency is \(1/6\), so the shortest positive period is six samples. Without a sample interval, physical Hz cannot be identified; infinitely many CT sinusoids produce those samples.

3. Why can the same rational transform be stable in one inverse and unstable in another?

For \(z/(z-2)\), the exterior ROC gives a causal growing sequence, whereas the interior ROC gives an anticausal decaying sequence toward negative infinity. Only the latter includes the unit circle. State the support and ROC before testing absolute summability; pole location alone is sufficient only after imposing causality.

4. Use Parseval to check the four-bin [1,1,0,0] example and explain why it is not a finite-energy signal.

The coefficient squared magnitudes are \(1/4,1/8,0,1/8\), summing to \(1/2\), equal to mean squared samples over a period. Repetition over infinitely many periods makes total energy infinite. A single isolated two-sample pulse instead has energy 2 and a continuous periodic DTFT.

5. Design an FIR notch at DC and a stable narrow resonance at π/2. What changes if the resonance radius reaches one?

Use the cascade \(H(z)=(1-z^{-1})/(1+r^2z^{-2})\), with \(0<r<1\). The zero at one rejects DC; poles at \(\pm jr\) create resonance. As \(r\to1\), decay slows and resonance sharpens. At one, the poles lie on the unit circle and the causal system fails BIBO stability; bounded resonant input can accumulate unbounded output.
