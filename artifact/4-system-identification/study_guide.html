1. Design the experiment around the intended use

Identification estimates a mathematical input-output mechanism from observed process data. In a discrete state-space model, \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k\) and \(y_k=Cx_k+Du_k\), the sample index is \(k\), the latent state is \(x_k\in\mathbb R^n\), input \(u_k\in\mathbb R^m\), and output \(y_k\in\mathbb R^r\). Consequently \(A,B,C,D\) have dimensions \(n\times n,n\times m,r\times n,r\times m\). The white-box versus black-box comparison concerns the origin and interpretation of a model's structure; a black-box state need not correspond to a particular tank level or temperature. Two state bases can describe the same measured behavior. Slide evidence: SIM p3, SIM p4.

Useful identification requires informative data, an appropriate model class, and a fitting algorithm. The workflow diagram routes failed validation back to different causes: collecting more of an uninformative experiment cannot repair absent excitation, while increasing model order cannot recover operating regimes that were never observed. The input panels on SIM p7 plot amplitude against time: a single step concentrates a change at one onset, a pulse returns to baseline, and a persistently switching sequence repeatedly probes dynamics. The relevant question is whether the inputs excite the timescales and directions required for the intended model, subject to process safety and experimental duration. Slide evidence: SIM p6, SIM p7.

Prediction, simulation, and control impose different demands on a model. Parsimony means favoring the simpler model among models that serve the same task; it does not justify ignoring reproducible dynamics. SIM uses matrix projections to infer a state sequence and regression to fit its dynamics; PEM starts from a parameterized predictor and reduces a prediction-error objective. A typical PEM criterion is \(V_N(\theta)=N^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^N\varepsilon_k(\theta)^T W\varepsilon_k(\theta)\), with parameter vector \(\theta\), prediction residual \(\varepsilon_k(\theta)=y_k-\hat y_{k|k-1}(\theta)\), positive-semidefinite output weighting matrix \(W\), and \(N\) training samples; \(\hat y_{k|k-1}\) uses information available before the output at sample \(k\). These are complementary strategies: a subspace estimate can provide an initial state-space model for further optimization. The deck's contrast between convex SIM and nonconvex PEM is a practical overview, rather than a universal theorem about every subspace variant or every prediction-error objective. Slide evidence: SIM p8, SIM p9, SIM p10, PEM p3.

Tutorial expansion / correction: Similarity transformations preserve input-output behavior; the explicit weighted PEM criterion, hybrid SIM-to-PEM initialization and qualifications about convexity add reasoning beyond the diagrams.

Study method

Before fitting, state the intended horizon, admissible input range, sampling interval, and validation operating regime. Those choices determine what “good” means.

Exam pitfall

A hidden state is a predictive coordinate. Do not assign physical meaning to a fitted matrix entry without an identified correspondence to physical variables.

2. Validate errors in time, not just a fit percentage

The deck defines \(\operatorname{MSE}=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\), \(\operatorname{NMSE}=\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2/\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2\), and \(R^2=1-\operatorname{NMSE}\), where \(N\) is the number of evaluated samples, \(y_i\) the measured reference, \(\hat y_i\) the corresponding model output, and \(\bar y=N^{-1}\sum_i y_i\). MSE retains squared output units; NMSE and \(R^2\) normalize against a constant-mean baseline. A negative \(R^2\) is possible and means that the chosen mean baseline has smaller squared error. A constant reference makes the NMSE denominator zero, so the normalized score is undefined. Slide evidence: SIM p11, SIM p12.

The repeated prediction overlay compares output amplitude vertically against time horizontally. Agreement in broad trends can conceal lag, attenuated peaks, or structured residuals, so visual overlap and scalar scores answer different questions. Define \(e_k=y_k-\hat y_k\) and its lag-\(\ell\) covariance \(\gamma_e(\ell)=\operatorname{Cov}(e_k,e_{k-\ell})\). Significant nonzero-lag residual correlation suggests remaining predictable structure. Residual-input correlation is also informative: with appropriate causal timing and experiment assumptions, correlation with past input suggests that input-driven dynamics remain unmodeled. Zero sample correlation alone does not prove independence or guarantee that all nonlinear dependence has been captured. Slide evidence: SIM p5, SIM p12.

The SIM p12 complexity graph places model complexity on the horizontal axis and prediction error vertically: training error falls as flexibility increases, while test error initially falls and then rises. Its minimum motivates validation-based order selection rather than minimizing training error. Evaluate on a withheld chronological segment, using only information available at each prediction origin. Reusing training observations to report fit understates generalization error. The AR(5) demonstration reports a high one-step score, but its regressor uses measured previous outputs and its code computes a score on the same series used to estimate the weights. It therefore demonstrates regression mechanics rather than proving autonomous simulation quality. When comparing MIMO models, report channel-specific errors: a large-variance output can dominate an unscaled aggregate loss and conceal failure on another channel. Slide evidence: PEM p8, SIM p11, SIM p12.

Tutorial expansion / correction: Chronological validation, residual-input checks, the constant-reference exception, and channel scaling are added diagnostic guidance. The AR(5) assessment explicitly qualifies the demonstration rather than changing the source.

Study method

Test a mean predictor, a parsimonious dynamic model, and a richer model on identical held-out times and horizons. Examine the residual sequence as well as the score.

Exam pitfall

“Uncorrelated error” is not the same as independent error. A good one-step training score is not evidence of reliable free-run dynamics.

3. CVA finds predictive coordinates by whitening past and future

Canonical variate analysis stacks historical measurements into past and future windows. Let \(Y_p\in\mathbb R^{d_p\times M}\) and \(Y_f\in\mathbb R^{d_f\times M}\) contain centered window vectors as columns, with \(M\) matched windows and \(d_p,d_f\) the respective window dimensions. The projection diagram seeks \(C_p=JY_p\) and \(C_f=LY_f\), where \(J,L\) contain projection weights and \(C_p,C_f\) are canonical coordinates. The arrows connect past information to future-predictive directions: CVA values a direction for its temporal correlation, rather than its raw variance. Slide evidence: SIM p14, SIM p15.

Define sample covariance blocks \(\Sigma_{pp}=Y_pY_p^T/(M-1)\), \(\Sigma_{ff}=Y_fY_f^T/(M-1)\), and \(\Sigma_{fp}=Y_fY_p^T/(M-1)\). Whitening leads to \[H=\Sigma_{ff}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{fp}\Sigma_{pp}^{-1/2}=USV^T,\qquad J=V^T\Sigma_{pp}^{-1/2},\quad L=U^T\Sigma_{ff}^{-1/2}.\] Here \(H\) is the whitened cross-covariance matrix, \(U,V\) its orthonormal singular-vector matrices, and \(S\) its diagonal singular-value matrix. On the supported nonsingular covariance subspaces, the canonical coordinates have identity covariance and cross-covariance \(S\); the singular values are canonical correlations. The covariance normalization must be applied consistently: once \(\Sigma_{pp}\) already includes \(1/(M-1)\), there is no second factor multiplying \(J\Sigma_{pp}J^T\). Slide evidence: SIM p16, SIM p17.

Retaining the strongest past canonical directions defines an estimated state matrix \(X\). The singular-value plot uses component index horizontally and correlation strength vertically; a sharp initial drop followed by a flatter tail suggests a possible separation between predictive state information and weak directions. Its knee is a candidate order, not an automatic decision rule. If \(X_-\) stacks estimated states at sample \(k\), \(X_+\) states at \(k+1\), \(U_-\) corresponding inputs and \(Y_-\) outputs, the regression is \[\begin{bmatrix}\hat A&\hat B\\\hat C&\hat D\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}X_+\\Y_-\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X_-\\U_-\end{bmatrix}^{+}.\] The superscript \(+\) denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse; hats mark fitted matrices. The slide's formula \((Q^TQ)^{-1}Q^T\), with \(Q\) a generic matrix, works only for full column rank. For wide or rank-deficient stacked regressors use an SVD-based pseudoinverse. For driven systems, practical subspace implementations must handle future-input effects; raw past-future output correlation alone is not a complete general MIMO identification algorithm. Slide evidence: SIM p17, SIM p18.

Tutorial expansion / correction: Centering, covariance normalization, pseudoinverse rank conditions, supported-subspace whitening, and treatment of future inputs clarify the schematic derivation.

Study method

Whitening removes the advantage of high-variance directions. Derive unit covariance first, then show that diagonal cross-covariance equals the singular values.

Exam pitfall

A canonical coordinate and a physical state are not automatically the same. A singular-value knee needs held-out validation and enough data relative to window dimension.

4. Sampling defines the dynamics you identify

A continuous model \(\dot x(t)=A_cx(t)+B_cu(t)\), \(y(t)=C_cx(t)+D_cu(t)\) describes variation at real time \(t\); subscripts \(c\) and \(d\) distinguish continuous and discrete matrices. For a fixed sampling interval \(T_s>0\) and an input held constant over each interval, the exact zero-order-hold relation is \[A_d=e^{A_cT_s},\qquad B_d=\left(\int_0^{T_s}e^{A_c\tau}\,d\tau\right)B_c,\qquad C_d=C_c,\quad D_d=D_c.\] The variable \(\tau\) is integration time and \(e^{A_cT_s}\) is a matrix exponential. SIM p19 uses a symbol that resembles the sample index in these expressions; the integration limit and exponential multiplier should be the sampling interval, not the running index. Slide evidence: SIM p19.

The hold diagrams plot signal value vertically against elapsed time horizontally. ZOH creates horizontal plateaus between samples, while FOH joins successive values with sloping segments. Neither curve reproduces every intersample feature of the original continuous signal; they encode different input reconstruction assumptions. A discrete model fitted to sampled data captures behavior at those sample times. Changing the sample interval changes its matrices, even if the underlying physical dynamics stay fixed. Continuous reconstruction also needs assumptions about sampling and matrix-logarithm branches; discrete poles alone do not uniquely reveal arbitrarily fast continuous oscillations. Slide evidence: SIM p19.

For a scalar decay \(\dot x=-\alpha x+bu\), \(\alpha>0\) is a decay rate and \(b\) an input coefficient. The sampled pole is \(a_d=e^{-\alpha T_s}\), and the discrete input coefficient is \(b_d=b(1-e^{-\alpha T_s})/\alpha\). Thus a pole close to one can mean a slow physical process or a short sampling interval. More samples per unit time improve time resolution but need not improve signal-to-noise ratio or independent information. Choose windows long enough to cover the process memory and an experiment long enough to reveal the relevant transients. Slide evidence: SIM p7, SIM p19.

Tutorial expansion / correction: The scalar example, aliasing qualification and corrected sampling-interval notation are tutorial additions. No MATLAB conversion is executed here.

Study method

Check units: an exponential argument is dimensionless, so a continuous rate matrix must be multiplied by a time interval.

Exam pitfall

Do not substitute the running index k for the fixed sampling interval. Do not interpret a near-unit discrete pole without knowing the sample period.

5. Polynomial structure determines the predictor and estimator

The general SISO structure separates measured dynamics and disturbance shaping: \[A(z)y_k=\frac{B(z)}{F(z)}u_k+\frac{C(z)}{D(z)}e_k,\qquad G(z)=\frac{B(z)}{A(z)F(z)},\quad H_e(z)=\frac{C(z)}{A(z)D(z)}.\] Here \(z^{-1}y_k=y_{k-1}\) is the delay action, \(A,B,C,D,F\) are scalar delay polynomials, \(y_k\) the output, \(u_k\) the exogenous input and \(e_k\) a zero-mean innovation; \(G\) and \(H_e\) are process and disturbance transfer functions. The symbol \(H_e\) is unrelated to the CVA matrix \(H\). AR uses \(A\), ARX uses \(A,B\), ARMA uses \(A,C\), and ARMAX uses \(A,B,C\), with remaining denominator polynomials equal to one. A Box-Jenkins structure separates process and noise denominators, while FIR models use only a finite input response. Slide evidence: PEM p4, PEM p5, PEM p11.

For an AR model of order \(p\), write \(y_k=c+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_jy_{k-j}+e_k\), where \(c\) is an intercept and \(\phi_j\) are recursion coefficients. Stack \(Y=[y_{p+1},\ldots,y_N]^T\), regressor rows \(\Phi_k=[1,y_{k-1},\ldots,y_{k-p}]\), and weights \(w=[c,\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_p]^T\). Least squares minimizes \(\|Y-\Phi w\|_2^2\); a numerically stable solve uses QR or SVD rather than explicitly inverting \(\Phi^T\Phi\). Polynomial coefficients and recursion coefficients have opposite signs when \(A(z)=1-\sum_j\phi_jz^{-j}\). An intercept is an additive term, not simply the constant coefficient of a monic delay polynomial. Slide evidence: PEM p5, PEM p7, PEM p8.

The sample-path plots on PEM p6 place sample index on the horizontal axis and output amplitude vertically. Repeated colored trajectories from the same initial values diverge under different noise realizations. The AR(3) recursion has characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^3-0.8\lambda^2+0.2\lambda-0.3\), whose poles are within the unit circle; its initial transient decays. The stated ARMA recursion has \(\lambda^2-0.9\lambda-0.1=(\lambda-1)(\lambda+0.1)\), so it contains a unit root; its wandering paths should not be described as a stationary ARMA example. The moving-average factor \(1+2z^{-1}\) is also noninvertible in that representation. Linearity of the dynamic system does not imply linearity in parameters of its prediction-error objective: AR and ARX have observed regressors, while MA innovations and general rational-model predictors usually require iterative estimation. Slide evidence: PEM p6, PEM p22, PEM p11.

Tutorial expansion / correction: The ARMA root analysis corrects the implied stationary example. The slide’s “all linear models need no iterations” claim is too broad. ACF threshold crossing alone does not determine AR order; consider PACF, information criteria and validation, since an AR ACF typically tails off.

Study method

Choose the process transfer and the disturbance transfer together. A model that forces both to share poles may be too restrictive even when its one-step fit looks good.

Exam pitfall

Linear in signals does not mean linear in unknown parameters. Never infer stationarity solely from a finite plotted trajectory or select AR order by the first ACF threshold crossing.

6. One-step prediction and simulation ask different questions

One-step prediction conditions on measured past outputs. Iterated multi-step prediction replaces unavailable future outputs with the model's own forecasts, while direct prediction estimates a separate mapping to a specified future horizon. The diagrams show measured versus predicted points across a vertical boundary at the forecast origin: the usable measured window shifts with each one-step evaluation, whereas the iterated path extends beyond that boundary with increasing reliance on earlier forecasts. A direct model can avoid some recursive error propagation, but the slide's “more accurate” statement is not a guarantee; data size, model class and forecast horizon determine the tradeoff. Slide evidence: PEM p9.

For \(y_{k+1}=ay_k+bu_k+e_{k+1}\), \(a,b\) are scalar coefficients and innovations \(e_k\) are independent, zero-mean with variance \(\sigma^2\). Given measured \(y_k\) and known future inputs, the \(h\)-step conditional mean is \[\hat y_{k+h|k}=a^hy_k+b\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}a^{h-1-j}u_{k+j},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(y_{k+h}-\hat y_{k+h|k})=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}a^{2j}.\] Here \(h\) is the positive forecast horizon and \(j\) a summation index. For \(|a|<1\) this variance is bounded as the horizon grows; a unit pole accumulates uncertainty linearly, and an unstable pole can amplify it exponentially. This isolates innovation accumulation; parameter estimation error adds further uncertainty. Slide evidence: PEM p9, PEM p10.

Free-run simulation propagates internal model state and specified exogenous inputs without repeatedly replacing predicted outputs by measured outputs. “No actual data as input” on PEM p10 refers to measured output feedback, not to removing the external input signal or initial conditions. Excellent one-step tracking can coexist with poor free-run behavior because measurements repeatedly reset the predictor. Stability is crucial for long simulation, but the claim that prediction has “no need” for stability needs qualification: a short-horizon predictor may be evaluable for an unstable plant, while long-horizon forecasts and the predictor's own error dynamics still require careful stability analysis. Slide evidence: PEM p8, PEM p10.

Tutorial expansion / correction: The explicit forecast/variance derivation, direct-prediction qualification and interpretation of simulation inputs extend and refine the diagrams.

Study method

Report at least one horizon matching the application. If the application is process optimization, validate free-run trajectories from declared initial conditions.

Exam pitfall

Initializing a free-run model is legitimate; continually feeding it measured outputs changes the task into prediction. Direct multi-step models are not universally superior.

7. Nonlinear identification separates static maps from memory

Hammerstein-Wiener modeling places static input and output nonlinearities around a linear dynamic block: \(v_k=f(u_k)\), \(s_k=G(z)v_k\), \(y_k=g(s_k)\). The functions \(f,g\) are memoryless maps, \(v_k\) the transformed input, \(s_k\) the internal linear-block output, and \(G\) the dynamic transfer operator. Hammerstein uses an input nonlinearity; Wiener uses an output nonlinearity. The diagram's block order matters: saturation before a lag can suppress the excitation entering the dynamics, while saturation after a lag clips the output of an already-evolving state. Arbitrary nonlinear dynamics need not admit this decomposition. Slide evidence: PEM p13.

A neural unit computes \(g(x)=\sigma(w^Tx+b)\), with input vector \(x\), weight vector \(w\), scalar bias \(b\), activation \(\sigma\), and scalar output \(g\). The worked sigmoid unit uses \(x=(1,4,3)^T\), \(w=(0.4,0.5,0.2)^T\), \(b=2\), giving \(w^Tx+b=5\) and \(\sigma(5)\approx0.993\). The activation graphs put preactivation vertically transformed by identity, ReLU, tanh or sigmoid against their horizontal scalar argument: linear activation preserves slope; ReLU has a zero negative branch; tanh and sigmoid saturate at large magnitudes. Universal approximation concerns representational capacity under appropriate domain and activation assumptions; it does not promise learnability from a finite dataset, extrapolation accuracy or stable recursion. Slide evidence: PEM p14, PEM p15.

Feedforward networks map supplied features to outputs, while recurrent or delayed-feedback structures carry memory. The NARX architecture combines delayed inputs and outputs; in a closed loop, predicted output feeds later predictions. The evaporator diagram identifies inputs F200, P100 and F3 and outputs L2, P2 and X2, with a physical feedback loop regulating separator level. Its coupled nonlinear behavior motivates MIMO identification, but feedback complicates naive interpretations of input-output correlation. The evaporator plots put sample index horizontally and channel value vertically: stepwise manipulated inputs produce smooth, coupled responses rather than instantaneous output copies. L2 remains near its controlled level between transients, while P2 and X2 show slower excursions. PEM p23 gives the data-to-cell-array conversion, delay specification, Bayesian-regularized training and held-out plotting workflow. Its three output overlays show blue predictions broadly following black observations, but L2 peak amplitudes and rapid changes are visibly less faithful than the smoother P2 trajectory; inspect channel-specific errors instead of assuming uniform success. Those diagrams and scripts specify an experiment; they do not provide numerical evidence that every output is accurately simulated. Use chronological validation and inspect whether the reported deployment mode matches the trained feedback arrangement. Slide evidence: PEM p16, PEM p17, PEM p18, PEM p19, PEM p20, PEM p23, SIM p21.

Tutorial expansion / correction: Capacity-versus-generalization, saturation effects and closed-loop validation are tutorial reasoning. The code is preserved as source material and has not been executed or verified against a particular MATLAB release.

Study method

Try a simple dynamic baseline before a large recurrent model. Use known actuator saturation or sensor transformations to motivate a structured nonlinearity.

Exam pitfall

A network’s approximation theorem does not establish recurrent stability. A randomly interleaved train/validation split can leak nearby temporal information.

Five synthesis challenges

Answers are tutorial derivations and critiques. Try a complete argument before opening each disclosure.

A two-output model has excellent aggregate R² but misses the smaller channel. What should change?

Report separate errors using each channel’s own reference variance, and examine residual correlation by channel. Check whether the training loss was dominated by the larger-amplitude output; rescaling can change the fitted compromise. Then compare chronological one-step and free-run validation, keeping the same test segment and initial conditions. A more complex model is justified only if improved performance persists on the intended task.

Why does the right-hand “ARMA” sample path wander, and can a high one-step score rescue stationarity?

Its recursion polynomial factors as \((\lambda-1)(\lambda+0.1)\), placing one pole at unity. The disturbance factor does not cancel that pole, so stochastic shocks accumulate. A one-step predictor can still track by using the latest observation, but this does not change the generating process’s stationarity. Under the displayed noise representation, the MA factor also has an inverse with an unstable causal pole. Stationarity and invertibility are separate checks.

A CVA singular-value knee suggests three states. Why is choosing n = 3 only a hypothesis?

Finite-window covariance estimates and noise can create or blur a knee. Confirm that windows capture relevant memory, are centered consistently and have adequate rank. Compare nearby orders on held-out trajectories and residuals. The retained state directions maximize predictive correlation in the selected representation; they need not be unique physical coordinates. A driven-data implementation must account for future inputs, and whitening may need regularization.

For a stable scalar system, derive how simulation uncertainty changes with horizon.

With \(y_{k+1}=ay_k+bu_k+e_{k+1}\), known inputs and independent innovations of variance \(\sigma^2\), the forecast error is \(\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}a^j e_{k+h-j}\). Independence removes cross terms, giving \(\sigma^2(1-a^{2h})/(1-a^2)\) for \(|a|<1\). It tends to \(\sigma^2/(1-a^2)\). At \(a=1\), the variance is \(h\sigma^2\). Here \(h\) is forecast horizon; uncertain coefficients and unknown future inputs create additional uncertainty beyond this calculation.

Would a bigger NARX network solve actuator saturation and poor free-run validation?

Not necessarily. An input saturation followed by approximately linear dynamics suggests a Hammerstein structure and may be learned more efficiently with fewer parameters. Increasing NARX capacity cannot recover unexcited regimes, prevent temporal leakage, or ensure stable recurrent behavior. Validate output feedback mode, initialization, chronological splits and channel-specific errors. Compare the structured nonlinear model and a linear baseline before accepting a larger recurrent model.
