1. Model the dependence, then choose the task

A time series is an ordered sequence whose observations can carry information about later observations. The scientific task is to characterize this dependence through a model of the generating mechanism, rather than merely fit a curve to unordered points. The introductory input-system-output diagram separates an external excitation from the transformation and its observed response. Forecasting uses the observed past to infer the future; system identification estimates the mechanism linking inputs to outputs; intervention analysis asks how a specified event changes the trajectory. Joint modeling of related variables can expose delayed interactions that separate univariate analyses miss, although predictive association alone does not establish causality.Slide evidence: Intro slide 3, Intro slide 4, Intro slide 5, Intro slide 7, Intro slide 8, Intro slide 9.

These tasks require distinct assumptions. A deterministic signal is specified without random uncertainty; a stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time. Weak stationarity requires a constant finite mean and covariance depending only on lag: \[\mathbb E[z_t]=\mu,\qquad \gamma(k)=\operatorname{Cov}(z_t,z_{t-k}),\qquad \gamma(0)<\infty.\] Here \(z_t\) is the random observation at integer time \(t\), \(\mu\) its mean, \(k\) a lag, and \(\gamma\) the autocovariance function. A system's time invariance concerns its response to a shifted input; a process's stationarity concerns its probability structure. Neither property is synonymous with the other. A dynamic system has memory and can still be time-invariant, as a constant-coefficient recursion demonstrates.Slide evidence: Intro slide 4, Intro slide 39.Tutorial extension: The formal weak-stationarity definition and the distinction between stationarity and time invariance are tutorial clarifications.

Forecasting is therefore a cycle of specification, parameter estimation, and diagnostic checking, with an uncertainty statement attached to each prediction. A binary intervention can encode a one-time pulse or a persistent step; the system's impulse response determines whether its output effect disappears, decays, or persists. Control instead selects an input to steer the output, potentially through feedback; the deck identifies this application but excludes control design from the course scope. The final classification slide should be read as several independent axes: linearity, time variation, time domain, input/output dimension, and stochastic stationarity.Slide evidence: Intro slide 6, Intro slide 8, Intro slide 10, Intro slide 39.Tutorial extension: Pulse-versus-step encoding and the response interpretation extend the intervention discussion.

Study trace · intervention versus forecast

First encode a campaign as a pulse if it occurs only once, or a step if a new regime begins. Next pass that input through the dynamic model. Finally forecast the remaining stochastic component and combine both contributions. Comparing the predicted paths with and without the intervention isolates the model's proposed effect; a causal interpretation requires assumptions about competing influences.

Exam pitfall · dynamic does not mean time-varying

A constant-coefficient system can retain past inputs while responding identically at every date. Conversely, a stable time-invariant filter does not make every possible input stationary. Stationarity must be checked for the resulting stochastic process under the stated input and initialization assumptions.

2. Signals are functions; systems are operators

A signal maps an index to an amplitude; a system maps an entire admissible signal to another signal. Continuous time uses a real index, while discrete time uses an integer index. Uniform sampling creates \(x[n]=x_c(t_0+nT)\), where \(x_c\) is a continuous-time signal, \(t_0\) the initial sampling time, \(T>0\) the sampling interval, and \(n\) an integer. Discrete time does not imply quantized amplitude. In \(y=H[x]\), \(H\) is an operator and the brackets do not generally denote scalar multiplication. The derivative block illustrates this distinction: each output value depends on how the input changes around the corresponding time.Slide evidence: Intro slide 12, Intro slide 13, Intro slide 14, Intro slide 15.

The delay algebra makes temporal dependence manipulable: \[Bz_t=z_{t-1},\qquad B^m z_t=z_{t-m},\qquad F=B^{-1},\qquad \nabla z_t=(1-B)z_t.\] Here \(B\) is the backward shift, \(F\) the forward shift on sequences where the inverse is defined, \(m\) a positive integer, and \(\nabla\) the backward difference. For an operator polynomial \(P(B)=\sum_{j=0}^{q}c_jB^j\), the integer \(q\) is its degree and \(c_j\) are fixed weights; applying it produces \(P(B)z_t=\sum_{j=0}^{q}c_jz_{t-j}\). Constant-coefficient delay polynomials commute under well-defined operations. An inverse polynomial may represent an infinite filter, and its existence as a causal stable filter requires more than a formal division symbol.Slide evidence: Intro slide 16, Intro slide 17.Tutorial extension: The general polynomial and cautions about operator inversion extend the slide notation.

Linearity and time invariance are two separate tests: \[H[\alpha x_1+\beta x_2]=\alpha H[x_1]+\beta H[x_2],\qquad H[S_\ell x]=S_\ell H[x].\] The signals \(x_1,x_2\) are arbitrary admissible inputs, \(\alpha,\beta\) scalars, and \(S_\ell x[n]=x[n-\ell]\) shifts a sequence by integer \(\ell\). In continuous time the same test uses a real shift. The paired diagrams move a gain or an adder across a linear block without changing the output; the LTI diagram also moves delays across it. A fixed gain, sum, or delay is linear; multiplication of two varying inputs is jointly nonlinear. Multiplication by a prescribed carrier is linear in the signal being modulated, but generally time-varying. This qualification improves the slide's blanket classification of modulation as nonlinear.Slide evidence: Intro slide 18, Intro slide 19, Intro slide 20, Intro slide 21, Intro slide 22, Intro slide 23.Tutorial extension: The fixed-carrier modulation qualification corrects the overly broad nonlinear classification on Intro slide 20.

Study trace · perform both system tests

For \(H[x][n]=n\,x[n]\), scaling and summing inputs commute with multiplication by \(n\), so the system is linear. However, \(H[S_\ell x][n]=n x[n-\ell]\), while \(S_\ell H[x][n]=(n-\ell)x[n-\ell]\). These differ for a nonzero shift, so it is time-varying. For \(H[x][n]=x[n]+c\), a fixed nonzero offset \(c\) violates the zero-input test and makes the map affine.

Exam pitfall · a delay symbol is not a transform variable

In a sequence equation, \(B\) acts on the sequence. Under a bilateral Z-transform, a unit delay corresponds to multiplication by \(z^{-1}\), where \(z\) is the complex transform variable. Finite records need a boundary convention; advancing the first or last sample does not magically recover unobserved data.

3. Impulse responses unify physical and stochastic filters

An impulse decomposes a discrete signal into weighted time shifts. Linearity lets a system respond separately to those components, and time invariance identifies each component response with a shifted impulse response. Thus, for a system initially at rest, \[y[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k]h[n-k],\qquad y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(\tau)h(t-\tau)\,d\tau.\] Here \(x\) is the input, \(y\) the output, \(h\) the impulse response, \(n,k\) integer indices, and \(t,\tau\) continuous times. The discrete impulse is one at zero and zero elsewhere; the continuous Dirac impulse is a generalized function with unit integral. The convolution diagram condenses all weighted, shifted responses into a single block. A causal response is zero before the impulse occurs.Slide evidence: Intro slide 11, Intro slide 34, Intro slide 35.Tutorial extension: The impulse-decomposition argument and initial-rest qualification expand the convolution statements.

The second deck applies exactly this mechanism to random shocks: \[z_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j a_{t-j},\qquad \psi_0=1,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(z_t)=\sigma_a^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j^2.\] Here \(a_t\) is zero-mean white noise with variance \(\sigma_a^2\), \(\mu\) the level, \(\psi_j\) fixed impulse-response weights, and \(j\) the shock's age. Independent identically distributed shocks, as assumed in the slides, are stronger than merely uncorrelated white noise. Shared past shocks create output dependence even when the shocks themselves are independent. Under square summability and a stationary infinite-past construction, the variance exists and the process is weakly stationary. Absolute summability, \(\sum_j|\psi_j|<\infty\), additionally gives bounded-input bounded-output stability and is sufficient for square summability.Slide evidence: Models slide 3, Models slide 4, Models slide 5.Tutorial extension: The variance formula, square-summability condition, and white-noise qualification are tutorial extensions.

Finite impulse response (FIR) filters use finitely many nonzero weights; infinite impulse response (IIR) filters retain an unbounded history. The guitar slide's arrows deserve literal reading: the input branches into an undelayed path and two successive unit delays; the delayed paths each have gain one half, all three paths sum, and the result passes through an \(N\)-sample delay. Its drawn relation is \(y[n]=x[n-N]+\tfrac12x[n-N-1]+\tfrac12x[n-N-2]\), where \(N\) is the final delay. There is no output-to-input feedback arrow in this figure. It illustrates a feedforward FIR network, whereas the standard Karplus-Strong mechanism named by the slide requires a feedback delay loop. No empirical graphs or numerical trend plots appear in either deck; the visual evidence consists of operator and signal-flow diagrams.Slide evidence: Intro slide 24, Models slide 5.Tutorial extension: The explicit equation is derived by tracing the guitar diagram; the missing-feedback observation is a tutorial correction.

Study trace · dependence from independent shocks

Let \(z_t=\mu+a_t+0.5a_{t-1}\). Only one shock is shared by adjacent observations, so \(\operatorname{Var}(z_t)=1.25\sigma_a^2\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(z_t,z_{t-1})=0.5\sigma_a^2\), and covariances beyond lag one are zero. Independence of the shocks does not imply independence of their overlapping filtered outputs.

Exam pitfall · sufficient is not necessary

The statement on Models slide 5 that failure of absolute summability automatically implies nonstationarity is too strong. For example, \(\psi_j=1/(j+1)\) is square-summable but not absolutely summable: filtering finite-variance white noise in the mean-square sense gives a weakly stationary process, although the filter is not BIBO stable. A nonstationary process can also have a well-defined mean; a random walk can have constant mean and time-growing variance.

4. Read equations as implementable signal flow

Constant-coefficient differential and difference equations encode the same organizing idea: a weighted combination of output changes is driven by a weighted combination of input changes. Using a consistent indexing convention, \[\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_k\frac{d^k y(t)}{dt^k}=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_k\frac{d^k x(t)}{dt^k},\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{N}a_k y[n-k]=\sum_{k=0}^{M}b_k x[n-k].\] Here \(a_k,b_k\) are constant coefficients, \(x\) the input, \(y\) the output, and \(N,M\) the highest output/input derivative or delay indices in the respective equation. For discrete time, \(a_0\ne0\) permits solving for the current output. The continuous derivative-chain figure sends differentiated inputs through gains to a sum and combines output derivative branches according to the differential equation. Such drawings express algebraic structure; practical realizations often use integrators to avoid repeated differentiation of noisy measurements.Slide evidence: Intro slide 25, Intro slide 26, Intro slide 27, Intro slide 28.Tutorial extension: The displayed equations use corrected, consistent coefficients and indexing.

The discrete direct-form diagram has an input delay chain, a separate output delay chain, tap gains, and summing junctions. Reading it with all output terms initially on the left gives \[y[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{M}\frac{b_k}{a_0}x[n-k]-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{a_k}{a_0}y[n-k].\] Every \(z^{-1}\) box stores one previous sample; output feedback creates recurrence and commonly an IIR response. Positive gain labels on a feedback drawing can instead denote coefficients after their signs have been absorbed into the recurrence. Consequently, determine the sign convention from the defining equation before copying a diagram into code. Initial conditions also matter: total output is the sum of zero-input response from the initial state and zero-state response from the driving signal. A linear differential equation with a fixed nonzero initial state generally defines an affine input-output map.Slide evidence: Intro slide 26, Intro slide 28, Intro slide 29.Tutorial extension: The solved recurrence and affine-map interpretation make the sign and initialization assumptions explicit.

Sampling a derivative motivates finite differences but does not make them exact identities for arbitrary smooth signals: \[y_c'(nT)\approx\frac{y[n]-y[n-1]}{T},\qquad y_c''(nT)\approx\frac{y[n]-2y[n-1]+y[n-2]}{T^2}.\] Here \(y_c\) is the continuous signal, \(y[n]=y_c(nT)\), \(T\) the sampling interval, and \(n\) the sample index. The first diagram subtracts a delayed branch before scaling by \(1/T\); cascading that operation gives the second diagram's tap weights \(1,-2,1\) and factor \(1/T^2\). Both backward formulas have first-order error at \(nT\) under sufficient smoothness; the second-difference expression is centered at \((n-1)T\), where it has second-order error. A physical sampling interval must be retained when units matter.Slide evidence: Intro slide 30, Intro slide 31, Intro slide 32, Intro slide 33.Tutorial extension: Approximation symbols, error orders, and evaluation-time distinctions correct and expand the finite-difference slides.

Study trace · from a differential equation to a recursion

Take \(y_c'(t)+\lambda y_c(t)=\kappa x_c(t)\), with decay rate \(\lambda>0\), input gain \(\kappa\), and sampling interval \(T>0\). A backward-difference approximation gives \(y[n]=\alpha y[n-1]+\beta x[n]\), where \(\alpha=1/(1+\lambda T)\) and \(\beta=\kappa T/(1+\lambda T)\). Since \(0<\alpha<1\), its zero-state impulse response is \(\beta\alpha^n\) for nonnegative \(n\), and zero otherwise. This is a discretization, not the exact sampled continuous-time solution.

Exam pitfall · repair notation before deriving

Intro slide 26 omits the input coefficients in its compact displayed sum; Intro slide 28 repeats and misindexes terms. Use the consistent equations above. Intro slide 31 and Intro slide 33 write finite-difference substitutions with equality, but their relationship to continuous derivatives is approximate. Zero initial conditions make the input-output map linear; they do not change whether the underlying differential equation is linear.

5. State compresses memory and extends models to many variables

A state is a sufficient summary of the past for predicting future evolution when future inputs are supplied. A high-order scalar equation can therefore be represented by a first-order vector equation. To avoid confusing state with the scalar input used earlier, denote the state by \(\mathbf s\): \[\dot{\mathbf s}(t)=A\mathbf s(t)+B_u\mathbf u(t),\qquad \mathbf y(t)=C\mathbf s(t)+D\mathbf u(t).\] Here \(\mathbf s\in\mathbb R^{n_s}\) is the state, \(\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^{m}\) the input, \(\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^{p}\) the output, \(A\in\mathbb R^{n_s\times n_s}\) the dynamics matrix, \(B_u\in\mathbb R^{n_s\times m}\) the input matrix, \(C\in\mathbb R^{p\times n_s}\) the measurement matrix, and \(D\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}\) the direct feedthrough matrix. The symbol \(B_u\) here is a matrix, distinct from the backward-shift operator \(B\). Constant matrices define a time-invariant realization; time-dependent matrices define a potentially time-varying one.Slide evidence: Intro slide 36, Intro slide 37.Tutorial extension: The state symbol is renamed to avoid confusing it with the input; the definition of a sufficient state is explanatory.

Discrete time replaces the derivative with a next-state update: \[\mathbf s[k+1]=A_d\mathbf s[k]+B_d\mathbf u[k],\qquad \mathbf y[k]=C_d\mathbf s[k]+D_d\mathbf u[k].\] The integer \(k\) indexes samples; the subscripted matrices have the same corresponding dimensions as in continuous time but describe discrete dynamics. One state coordinate can store a previous output and another a previous input. Thus a vector model can represent a scalar input-output system; state dimension does not determine the number of measured variables. Multiple input and output coordinates extend the same framework to MIMO systems, where cross-coupling matrices express how one channel influences another over time.Slide evidence: Intro slide 38, Intro slide 9.Tutorial extension: The discussion of state dimension versus input/output dimension expands the state-space equations.

With zero initial state, eliminating the internal state yields a transfer matrix: \[G(z)=C_d(zI-A_d)^{-1}B_d+D_d.\] Here \(z\) is the complex Z-transform variable, \(I\) the identity matrix of size \(n_s\), and \(G\) maps transformed inputs to outputs wherever the inverse exists. For a discrete impulse, \(H[0]=D_d\) and \(H[j]=C_dA_d^{j-1}B_d\) for integer \(j\ge1\), where \(H[j]\) is the matrix impulse response. The feedthrough path is instantaneous; the state path carries memory. An autonomous system has no external driving input, while a nonzero initial state can still generate output.Slide evidence: Intro slide 35, Intro slide 36, Intro slide 37, Intro slide 38.Tutorial extension: The transfer-matrix elimination and matrix impulse-response formulas are derived extensions.

Study trace · one recursion, two equivalent descriptions

For \(y[k]=\alpha y[k-1]+\beta u[k]\), define \(s[k]=y[k-1]\). Then \(s[k+1]=\alpha s[k]+\beta u[k]\) and \(y[k]=\alpha s[k]+\beta u[k]\). Hence \(A_d=C_d=\alpha\) and \(B_d=D_d=\beta\), all scalar matrices. The resulting transfer is \(G(z)=\beta/(1-\alpha z^{-1})\), agreeing with the delay-polynomial recursion. The direct term appears because the chosen output uses the current input.

Exam pitfall · internal stability and observed stability differ

If all eigenvalues of \(A_d\) have magnitude below one, the finite-dimensional discrete realization is internally asymptotically stable and has a stable input-output response. The converse can fail when unstable internal modes are unobservable or unreachable. For continuous time, the corresponding internal condition is strictly negative real parts, not eigenvalue magnitudes below one.

6. AR, MA, ARMA, and integration organize stochastic memory

Regression becomes autoregression when the predictors are the series' own past values. Center the series as \(\widetilde z_t=z_t-\mu\), where \(\mu\) is its stationary mean. The main model families can then be written together: \[\begin{aligned}\text{AR}(p):\quad &\phi(B)\widetilde z_t=a_t,\\ \text{MA}(q):\quad &\widetilde z_t=\theta(B)a_t,\\ \text{ARMA}(p,q):\quad &\phi(B)\widetilde z_t=\theta(B)a_t,\\ \phi(B)&=1-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_iB^i,\qquad \theta(B)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_jB^j.\end{aligned}\] Here \(p,q\) are the AR and MA orders, \(\phi_i\) coefficients of past observations, \(\theta_j\) coefficients of past shocks using the deck's minus-sign convention, \(B\) the delay operator, and \(a_t\) zero-mean white noise with variance \(\sigma_a^2\). AR memory is recurrent output feedback; MA memory is a finite weighted shock history. A statistical MA model does not mean an ordinary rolling average of observed values.Slide evidence: Models slide 6, Models slide 7, Models slide 9, Models slide 10.

A causal stationary ARMA process has the innovation-filter form \(\widetilde z_t=\psi(B)a_t\), with \(\psi(B)=\theta(B)/\phi(B)\). For a reduced model with no common polynomial factors, zeros of \(\phi\) must lie outside the unit circle in the complex \(B\)-plane for a causal stable representation. Invertibility instead requires zeros of \(\theta\) outside that circle, permitting recovery of innovations from observed history. Finite MA processes are stationary with finite-variance white noise regardless of invertibility. Generically, estimating mean, variance, and coefficients requires \(p+2\), \(q+2\), or \(p+q+2\) parameters for AR, MA, and ARMA respectively. In an uncentered AR equation the intercept is \(c=\mu(1-\sum_i\phi_i)\), where \(c\) is a constant intercept; it is generally different from the mean.Slide evidence: Models slide 5, Models slide 8, Models slide 9, Models slide 10.Tutorial extension: Causality and invertibility root conditions, polynomial reduction, and the intercept-mean relation extend the slides.

Integration extends these stationary models to series made stationary by differencing: \[\phi(B)(1-B)^d z_t=c+\theta(B)a_t.\] Here \(d\) is a nonnegative integer differencing order and \(c\) is the intercept in the differenced equation; this is an ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) model. A random walk \(z_t=z_{t-1}+a_t\) has \(d=1\): differencing gives white noise, whereas summing the shocks accumulates uncertainty. With fixed initial value \(z_0\), its variance after \(t\) steps is \(t\sigma_a^2\), so a constant mean does not make it stationary. Differencing is appropriate for this integration mechanism, but it is not a universal cure for structural breaks, changing variance, or deterministic trends. ARIMA is only introduced as a reading topic in the deck; this equation is an explanatory extension.Slide evidence: Intro slide 16, Models slide 11.Tutorial extension: The ARIMA equation, random-walk variance, and limitations of differencing expand the assigned reading topic.

Study trace · one-step and multi-step prediction

For the centered AR(1) model \(\widetilde z_t=\rho\widetilde z_{t-1}+a_t\), assume \(|\rho|<1\) and independent innovations. Given history through time \(t\), the \(h\)-step forecast is \(\widehat z_{t+h\mid t}=\mu+\rho^h(z_t-\mu)\), and its error variance is \(\sigma_a^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\rho^{2j}\), where \(h\) is a positive forecast horizon. The forecast returns toward the mean while uncertainty approaches the stationary variance. For a random walk, the point forecast stays at \(z_t\) and error variance grows as \(h\sigma_a^2\).

Exam pitfall · stationarity, invertibility, and signs

For AR(1), the root of \(1-\rho B\) is \(1/\rho\), so \(|\rho|<1\) means the polynomial root lies outside the unit circle. For the deck's MA(1), \(z_t-\mu=a_t-\theta a_{t-1}\), lag-one covariance is \(-\theta\sigma_a^2\). Software using a plus sign will use the opposite coefficient. A canceled AR/MA factor can also hide redundant parameters; root tests should be applied after reduction.

7. Separate the driven response from structured noise

An observed output can reflect both known external inputs and unobserved shocks. A rational transfer model describes the driven component through a delayed input and recurrent output dynamics: \[\delta(B)Y_t=\omega(B)B^bX_t,\qquad \nu(B)=\frac{\omega(B)B^b}{\delta(B)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\nu_jB^j.\] Here \(X_t\) is an external input, \(Y_t\) the deterministic driven output in this equation, \(b\ge0\) an integer dead time, \(\delta\) an output-dynamics polynomial, \(\omega\) an input polynomial, and \(\nu_j\) impulse-response weights. With a causal proper delay-series expansion, the first \(b\) weights are zero. The slides use \(\Omega(B)=\omega(B)B^b\) to absorb the explicit delay. Stability requires absolutely summable weights; in a reduced causal rational representation this corresponds to denominator roots outside the unit circle in the \(B\)-plane.Slide evidence: Models slide 12, Models slide 13, Models slide 14.

The alternative difference-operator form uses \(\nabla=1-B\), so substituting \(B=1-\nabla\) is algebraically exact between discrete representations. It should not be confused with replacing a continuous derivative by a scaled finite difference, which is an approximation. For example, \[(1+\xi\nabla)Y_t=\eta X_{t-b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y_t=\frac{\xi}{1+\xi}Y_{t-1}+\frac{\eta}{1+\xi}X_{t-b}.\] Here \(\xi,\eta\) are scalar parameters, \(b\) the input delay, and \(1+\xi\ne0\). A low-order numerator and denominator can approximate more complex inertia, but low order alone does not establish a good model. Its adequacy must be assessed against the dependence remaining in the residuals.Slide evidence: Intro slide 16, Models slide 12, Models slide 13.Tutorial extension: The first-order equivalence is a worked algebraic derivation; the derivative-versus-difference distinction is a clarification.

The final diagram has two parallel causal paths: external input passes through a dynamic-system filter, innovations pass through a noise filter, and an adder combines their outputs: \[Y_t=\nu(B)X_t+N_t=\frac{\omega(B)B^b}{\delta(B)}X_t+\frac{\theta(B)}{\phi(B)}a_t.\] Here \(N_t\) is the disturbance process, \(a_t\) its innovations, and \(\phi,\theta\) its AR and MA polynomials; \(Y_t\) now denotes the full noisy observation. Means or deterministic offsets are assumed removed or modeled separately. The slides assume the input and disturbance processes are independent. Estimation must therefore identify both the driven response \(\nu\) and the disturbance filter \(\psi=\theta/\phi\). Persistent residual correlation signals missing noise structure, while residual association with the input can signal missing dynamics or a violated independence assumption.Slide evidence: Models slide 15, Models slide 16.Tutorial extension: The residual diagnostic interpretations extend the two-filter diagram and estimation discussion.

Study trace · separate pulse response from disturbance

Let \(\nu(B)=\kappa B^2/(1-\rho B)\) with \(|\rho|<1\), input gain \(\kappa\), and two-sample delay. Expanding the denominator gives weights \(\nu_0=\nu_1=0\), \(\nu_2=\kappa\), \(\nu_3=\kappa\rho\), and \(\nu_{2+j}=\kappa\rho^j\) for nonnegative \(j\). A unit pulse produces this delayed decaying sequence. If disturbance is AR(1), \(N_t=\alpha N_{t-1}+a_t\) with \(|\alpha|<1\), subtracting the modeled pulse response leaves correlated \(N_t\); only \(a_t=N_t-\alpha N_{t-1}\) is the white innovation.

Exam pitfall · noise is not necessarily white

A residual disturbance can be colored even when its innovations are white. Input-noise dependence, as can occur when a controller responds to disturbances, violates the simple independence assumption and can compromise an identification method that relies on it. Forecasting also needs a plan for future external inputs: known, separately predicted, or scenario-based. Gaussian probability limits require a distributional assumption beyond merely knowing a variance.

Check Your Understanding

Each problem joins concepts from several themes. Work out the reasoning before revealing the answer. Parameters below are assumed known, so forecast variances do not include estimation uncertainty.

1. Can a linear time-invariant model generate a nonstationary series with a constant mean?

Yes. Let \(z_t=z_{t-1}+a_t\), where \(a_t\) are independent zero-mean shocks with variance \(\sigma_a^2\), and fix \(z_0=0\). The recurrence has constant coefficients and its zero-state input-output map is linear. But \(z_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}a_i\), so \(\mathbb E[z_t]=0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(z_t)=t\sigma_a^2\). The variance depends on time and the impulse response does not decay. Differencing gives \((1-B)z_t=a_t\), a stationary process. Time invariance describes the operator; stationarity describes the output distribution. A nonzero fixed initial state adds a zero-input response and changes the total input-output map to an affine one.

2. Derive the first impulse weights, variance, and invertibility of an ARMA(1,1) model.

Take \((1-0.8B)(z_t-\mu)=(1-0.4B)a_t\), where \(B\) delays one sample, \(\mu\) is the mean, and \(a_t\) is white noise of variance \(\sigma_a^2\). Expanding \(\psi(B)=(1-0.4B)/(1-0.8B)\) gives \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\psi_j=0.4(0.8)^{j-1}\) for \(j\ge1\). Thus the first four weights are \(1,0.4,0.32,0.256\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(z_t)=\sigma_a^2[1+0.16/(1-0.64)]=13\sigma_a^2/9\). The AR root is \(1.25\), giving a causal stationary representation, and the MA root is \(2.5\), giving invertibility. Finite AR order does not imply finite impulse memory.

3. How does a delayed external pulse affect a forecast when the disturbance is correlated?

Let \(Y_t=\kappa B^2(1-\rho B)^{-1}X_t+N_t\) and \(N_t=\alpha N_{t-1}+a_t\), with \(|\rho|,|\alpha|<1\). Here \(\kappa\) is input gain, \(\rho\) driven-response persistence, \(\alpha\) noise persistence, and independent innovations \(a_t\) have variance \(\sigma_a^2\). Given known input history and future input scenario, compute the driven component \(m_t=\nu(B)X_t\) and infer \(N_t=Y_t-m_t\). The \(h\)-step forecast is \(m_{t+h}+\alpha^hN_t\), with innovation variance \(\sigma_a^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\alpha^{2j}\). A unit pulse at time \(t\) first contributes \(\kappa\) at \(t+2\), then \(\kappa\rho\), and so on. Uncertain future inputs add forecast uncertainty beyond this conditional innovation variance.

4. Build a state realization for an AR(2) recursion and recover its first impulse weights.

Consider \(y[k]=a_1y[k-1]+a_2y[k-2]+u[k]\), where \(a_1,a_2\) are fixed recurrence coefficients and \(u\) is the input. Set \(\mathbf s[k]=(y[k-1],y[k-2])^\mathsf T\). Then \[A_d=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&a_2\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad B_d=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad C_d=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&a_2\end{bmatrix},\quad D_d=1.\] The state and output equations are those in Theme 5. For zero initial state and a unit impulse input, \(h[0]=1\), \(h[1]=a_1\), and \(h[2]=a_1^2+a_2\). The transfer is \(G(z)=1/(1-a_1z^{-1}-a_2z^{-2})\). Eigenvalues solve \(\lambda^2-a_1\lambda-a_2=0\); both must lie inside the unit circle for internal stability. The corresponding nonzero roots in the delay-variable polynomial are their reciprocals. Two states do not make this a two-output system.

5. Does differencing remove dependence, and what does it do to measurement noise?

Suppose \(z_t=\beta t+\varepsilon_t\), where \(\beta\) is a fixed slope and \(\varepsilon_t\) is IID zero-mean noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). The difference \(w_t=(1-B)z_t=\beta+\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\) has constant mean \(\beta\), variance \(2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), and lag-one covariance \(-\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). It is stationary but has lag-one correlation \(-1/2\); differencing created dependence from overlapping noise terms. In the deck's convention it is MA(1) with coefficient one, on the invertibility boundary. If estimating a physical derivative, divide \(w_t\) by sampling interval \(T\); its noise variance becomes \(2\sigma_\varepsilon^2/T^2\). Smaller sampling intervals can amplify derivative noise even while reducing deterministic approximation error.
