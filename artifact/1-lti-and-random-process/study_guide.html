1. Fit a waveform, identify a mechanism

The curve-fitting sequence cleans and smooths a measured waveform before adding polynomial and harmonic basis functions. With fixed frequencies, a model such as \(y(t)=c_0+c_1t+c_2\cos(\omega t)+c_3\sin(\omega t)+\varepsilon(t)\) is linear in coefficients even though it is nonlinear in time: \(t\) is time, \(y\) the measurement, \(c_i\) fitted coefficients, \(\omega\) angular frequency, and \(\varepsilon\) the residual. The plots show a rising baseline with oscillation superimposed; adding harmonics improves phase and waveform shape, but additional flexibility alone does not demonstrate out-of-sample forecasting accuracy. The vertical axis is observed amplitude and the horizontal axis advances through the record. [Curve fitting p3, Curve fitting p4, Curve fitting p5, Curve fitting p6, Curve fitting p7, Curve fitting p8]

System identification asks a different question: how does a manipulated input generate an output through dynamics and disturbances? The eight-state example displays markedly different behavior as the time axis expands: fast oscillations coexist with slower settling modes. Decomposing the response exposes these modes, while the designed inputs shorten the illustrated settling time from approximately 60 s to 1 s and then 0.5 s. Those are examples for the stated model; they do not establish unlimited achievable speed under actuator limits. For a stable continuous system \(\dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x+B\mathbf u,\;\mathbf y=C\mathbf x\) and constant input, equilibrium gives \(\mathbf y_\infty=-CA^{-1}B\mathbf u\) when \(A\) is invertible. Here \(\mathbf x,\mathbf u,\mathbf y\) are state, input, and output vectors; \(A,B,C\) are compatible system matrices. [Curve fitting p10, Curve fitting p11, Curve fitting p12, Curve fitting p13, Curve fitting p14, Curve fitting p15]Tutorial extension / qualification: The equilibrium derivation and actuator-limit qualification are added reasoning. The autonomous equation on Curve fitting p10 has no input; calling its output a step response requires an unshown input or an equivalent initial-state preparation.

The overview broadens the objective from forecasting to fault detection, clustering, and dimensionality reduction. Its plant traces have different scales and temporal patterns; an apparent lag relationship must be interpreted after accounting for units, operating changes, and common drivers. Battery degradation has a downward long-term trend, whereas weather traces combine fluctuating levels with local regularity. Choose the target and evaluation horizon before choosing model complexity; hold out later times so preprocessing and parameter selection cannot learn the future. [Time Series Overview p3, Time Series Overview p4, Time Series Overview p5, Time Series Overview p6, Time Series Overview p20, Time Series Overview p21]Tutorial extension / qualification: Chronological evaluation and leakage prevention are tutorial guidance.

Study strategy

Construct a basis matrix with constant, time, sine, and cosine columns; compare its residual structure against a dynamic model that includes measured inputs.

Exam pitfall

A visually excellent fit can still extrapolate poorly. Identify which quantities are manipulated, measured, hidden, or random.

2. Sampling changes what can be inferred

A discrete-time signal is indexed by integers; quantization separately restricts its amplitude levels. The impulse, step, ramp, exponential, and sinusoidal plots provide a vocabulary for building a record. A delay shifts stems to the right without changing amplitude; reversal reflects the index axis. Negative exponential bases alternate sign, while the magnitude of the base controls growth or decay. Time scaling must respect the integer lattice: downsampling discards samples, and upsampling inserts intermediate index positions rather than creating measured values. [DT Review p3, DT Review p4, DT Review p5, DT Review p6, DT Review p16, DT Review p18, DT Review p19, DT Review p20, DT Review p22, DT Review p23]Tutorial extension / qualification: For arbitrary discrete sequences, fractional arguments need an explicitly defined interpolation rule. DT Review p16 should say amplification requires absolute gain greater than one, rather than merely greater than zero.

Sampling identifies frequencies only modulo the sampling rate. With \(F_s=1/T_s\) samples per second and continuous frequency \(F\) in hertz, discrete angular frequency is \(\omega=2\pi F/F_s\) radians per sample, and \(\omega+2\pi m\) gives the same samples for any integer \(m\); \(T_s\) is the sample interval. DT Review plots at 40 Hz show 10 Hz and 50 Hz cosines passing through the same stems, making aliasing an observational ambiguity. An anti-alias filter must act before sampling. The practice link sends 10,000 bits/s with 1,024 levels: ten bits per sample imply 1,000 samples/s and a 500 Hz folding frequency. The 300 Hz component survives, while 900 Hz folds to 100 Hz. [DT Review p8, DT Review p9] [Sampling Practice p1, Sampling Practice p2, Sampling Practice p3, Sampling Practice p4]

Quantization trades bitrate against amplitude error. Under the endpoint-inclusive convention used in the practice definitions, \(\Delta=(x_{\max}-x_{\min})/(L-1)\) where \(\Delta\) is the level spacing, \(L\) the number of levels, and \(x_{\min},x_{\max}\) the minimum and maximum represented amplitudes. If a conservative range of -5 to 5 is chosen for the two-tone signal, the spacing is \(10/1023\); the assumed range must be stated because the problem does not explicitly fix converter full scale. Changing the D/A period after sampling rescales reconstructed frequencies: it cannot undo aliases already created by the A/D. Likewise, a finite-energy sequence has zero long-run average power, while a nonzero periodic sequence generally has finite positive power and infinite energy. [Sampling Practice p1, Sampling Practice p5, Sampling Practice p6] [DT Review p10, DT Review p11, DT Review p12]Tutorial extension / qualification: The conservative converter range is an assumption; some converter conventions use range divided by L, so do not mix definitions.

Study strategy

At 200 samples/s, 50 Hz maps to pi/2 and 125 Hz maps to 1.25 pi, equivalent to -0.75 pi. Preserve the sign for sine waves.

Exam pitfall

Nyquist rate belongs to the analog bandwidth; folding frequency belongs to the converter. A reconstruction filter cannot distinguish two signals with identical samples.

3. Convolution is weighted overlap with a time origin

Every discrete sequence can be decomposed into shifted impulses. For an initially resting LTI system, each impulse produces a shifted copy of the same impulse response, so superposition gives\[x[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k]\delta[n-k],\qquad y[n]=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k]h[n-k].\]Here \(x[n]\) is input, \(y[n]\) output, \(h[n]\) impulse response, \(\delta[n]\) the unit impulse, \(n\) the output index, and \(k\) the summation index. The diagrams are computational instructions: fold \(h[k]\) to \(h[-k]\) then shift by \(n\); multiply only aligned stems and sum their products. A shift of the kernel changes alignment, not its values. [LTI Analysis p6, LTI Analysis p8, LTI Analysis p9, LTI Analysis p10, LTI Analysis p11]

The worked four-by-four example yields seven values, \(1,4,8,8,3,-2,-1\); its origin is at the second output value because the impulse response begins at index -1. The output peak appears when several positive products overlap; the negative tail follows overlap with the negative end of the kernel. The small-support example has \(f[-2]=f[-1]=2\) and \(g[2]=2,\;g[3]=1\) with all other samples zero, so output support is 0 through 2 and values are 4, 6, 2. Support endpoints add under convolution; lengths add and subtract one when the endpoint samples are nonzero. [LTI Analysis p12, LTI Analysis p13, LTI Analysis p14, LTI Analysis p15, LTI Analysis p16, LTI Analysis p17, LTI Analysis p18, LTI Analysis p19, LTI Analysis p20, LTI Analysis p23, LTI Analysis p25, LTI Analysis p26]

The scanned block diagrams show cascade impulse responses convolving and parallel responses adding; direct-form rearrangements can share delay memory while preserving the ideal transfer. The square-root iteration shown nearby is nonlinear, so its convergent iteration must not be mistaken for an LTI convolution example. [System Responses p29, System Responses p30, System Responses p31, System Responses p32, System Responses p33, System Responses p34, System Responses p35, System Responses p36]The representations chart links a difference equation, signal-flow diagram, impulse response, transfer function, pole-zero geometry, and frequency response. For zero initial conditions, transforming the convolution gives \(Y(z)=H(z)X(z)\) where \(z\) is the complex transform variable and \(X,Y,H\) are input transform, output transform, and system transfer function. Evaluating at \(z=e^{j\omega}\) gives frequency response only when the unit circle lies in the region of convergence; \(j^2=-1\) and \(\omega\) is angular frequency. A pole near the unit circle can create a large response near its angle; zeros can suppress frequencies. A rational expression without its causality or convergence convention does not uniquely specify a sequence. [LTI representations p1] [DT Review p30, DT Review p31, DT Review p32, DT Review p33]Tutorial extension / qualification: The region-of-convergence and pole-geometry qualifications extend the chart.

Study strategy

Use support before arithmetic: the finite-support example needs only three output samples. Then verify both the value sequence and its first index.

Exam pitfall

An array result from convolution omits the physical time origin. A sequence with the right values at the wrong indices is still wrong.

4. Initial state, forcing, and characteristic modes

The scanned System Responses deck walks through the same distinction with a second-order recurrence, computing both total and zero-state response constants from boundary samples. [System Responses p3, System Responses p4, System Responses p7, System Responses p13, System Responses p14, System Responses p18, System Responses p20, System Responses p21]The physically meaningful response split is \(y[n]=y_{zi}[n]+y_{zs}[n]\) where \(y_{zi}\) is the response to initial memory with zero input and \(y_{zs}=x*h\) is the response to input with zero initial memory. The homogeneous-plus-particular split solves the equation algebraically; its homogeneous constants generally absorb boundary adjustments introduced by the chosen particular solution. Consequently, natural modes can appear inside a zero-state response. A fixed nonzero initial state adds an input-independent term, so linear superposition of total outputs needs care. [LTI Analysis p4, LTI Analysis p5, LTI Analysis p46, LTI Analysis p49, LTI Analysis p51, LTI Analysis p52, LTI Analysis p55]Tutorial extension / qualification: LTI Analysis p5 repeats “zero-input” for the forced component in its parenthetical note; the intended physical second component is zero-state.

For a constant-coefficient recurrence, substitute a trial mode \(\lambda^n\) to obtain the characteristic polynomial, where \(\lambda\) is a characteristic root and \(n\) the sample index. Distinct roots contribute \(C_i\lambda_i^n\) with constants \(C_i\) fixed by initial conditions. A repeated root of multiplicity \(r\) contributes powers \(n^q\lambda^n\) for \(q=0,\ldots,r-1\); conjugate roots \(\rho e^{\pm j\beta}\) contribute a real damped sinusoid, with \(\rho\) the modulus and \(\beta\) angle. Roots within the unit circle decay; negative roots alternate; roots outside it grow. Resonance between an input mode and a homogeneous root requires a polynomial factor in the particular-solution trial. [LTI Analysis p29, LTI Analysis p30, LTI Analysis p31, LTI Analysis p35, LTI Analysis p36, LTI Analysis p37, LTI Analysis p38, LTI Analysis p59, LTI Analysis p60]

Cross-check closed forms against the first iterative samples. For \(y[n]-0.6y[n-1]-0.16y[n-2]=5x[n]\) at rest, an impulse gives \(h[0]=5,\;h[1]=3,\;h[2]=2.6\) and\[h[n]=\bigl[(-0.2)^n+4(0.8)^n\bigr]u[n].\]Here \(u[n]\) is the unit step; the two characteristic roots are -0.2 and 0.8. [LTI Analysis p41, LTI Analysis p42, LTI Analysis p44, LTI Analysis p45]Tutorial correction: p45 prints a positive 0.2 in one fitting equation, but the correct relation is \(3=-0.2C_1+0.8C_2\); its final weights 1 and 4 are correct. The total-response example on p50 changes the coefficient 0.6 to 0.16 while reusing the previous response; these do not describe the same system. The rendered input on p47-48 is \(4^{-n}u[n]\) (an exponential), rather than \((4-n)u[n]\). The preserved slides let you audit these differences. [LTI Analysis p33, LTI Analysis p47, LTI Analysis p48, LTI Analysis p50] [DT Review p53, DT Review p64, DT Review p65, DT Review p67]

Study strategy

Always compute two or three samples from the original recurrence before accepting a closed form. This detects sign, origin, and copied-coefficient errors.

Exam pitfall

Zero-input is defined by a physical experiment; homogeneous is defined by an equation. Do not assume their constants match after selecting a particular solution.

5. A random process has both time and ensemble axes

A random process \(X(t,s)\) assigns a waveform to outcome \(s\) of an experiment; \(t\) is time. Fixing \(s\) selects one realization, fixing \(t\) gives a random variable across outcomes, and fixing both gives a number. The die example mixes four constant waveforms with two ramps, \(\pm t/2\); increasing separation of the ramp branches widens ensemble spread over time even though their average remains zero. Probability statements concern the ensemble, so a smooth realization does not imply a nonrandom process. The random-phase sinusoid is a particularly useful counterexample: randomness in its phase can coexist with perfectly regular temporal structure. [Random Process p2, Random Process p3, Random Process p6, Random Process p7, Random Process p9, Random Process p24, Random Process p26, Random Process p27]

Second-order summaries separate level from dependence:\[\mu_X(t)=\mathbb E[X(t)],\quad R_X(t_1,t_2)=\mathbb E[X(t_1)X(t_2)],\quad C_X(t_1,t_2)=R_X(t_1,t_2)-\mu_X(t_1)\mu_X(t_2).\]Here \(\mathbb E\) is expectation, \(\mu_X\) mean, \(R_X\) autocorrelation, \(C_X\) autocovariance, and \(t_1,t_2\) two times. Variance is \(C_X(t,t)\); for positive variances, correlation coefficient divides covariance by the product of standard deviations. The die ensemble gives zero mean but \(C_X(t_1,t_2)=40/6+t_1t_2/12\); its changing diagonal variance disproves weak stationarity. An independent noise process adds no cross-covariance with a desired signal, but it can add a product of means to cross-correlation. Uncorrelatedness is weaker than independence except under suitable jointly Gaussian assumptions. [Random Process p17, Random Process p19, Random Process p20, Random Process p21, Random Process p22, Random Process p23, Random Process p29, Random Process p30, Random Process p31, Random Process p33, Random Process p36, Random Process p37]Tutorial extension / qualification: The finite-variance and positive-denominator qualifications make the correlation statements precise.

Weak stationarity requires finite second moments, constant mean, and covariance depending only on lag \(\tau=t_2-t_1\); strict stationarity preserves all finite-dimensional distributions under a common shift. A uniform phase eliminates absolute-time terms in sinusoidal covariance, whereas random amplitude alone generally leaves a product of cosines that depends on both times. The six-function example with constants ±6 and sinusoids ±3 sin(t), ±3 cos(t) has \(R_Y(\tau)=12+3\cos\tau\) but its possible amplitudes change with time, demonstrating WSS without strict stationarity. Strict stationarity implies WSS only if second moments exist. Gaussian processes are special: their mean and covariance determine their finite-dimensional laws. [Random Process p26, Random Process p27, Random Process p28, Random Process p59, Random Process p63, Random Process p64, Random Process p66, Random Process p68, Random Process p69, Random Process p70, Random Process p71, Random Process p72, Random Process p77, Random Process p78]Tutorial extension / qualification: The p72 intermediate numerator contains 25 where the constant squared should be 36; its final 72+18 cos(tau) numerator is consistent with the stated ±6 ensemble. The Gaussian implication is an added synthesis.

Study strategy

For each claimed stationary process, check the diagonal covariance first, then lag dependence, then decide whether second moments suffice for the requested claim.

Exam pitfall

One trajectory is not an ensemble. Constant mean alone proves neither WSS nor strict stationarity.

6. Filtering, accumulation, and Markov memory

The IID and Bernoulli examples start with independent draws; accumulating them creates dependent levels. If \(I_n\) is an IID Bernoulli indicator with success probability \(p\) and \(S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i\) counts successes, then \(\mathbb E S_n=np\) and \(\operatorname{Var}S_n=np(1-p)\). Here \(n\) is trial count and \(i\) the summation index. The count plot never decreases because each increment is zero or one, while the signed walk built from \(D_n=2I_n-1\) can move in both directions. For the symmetric walk with step size \(h\) and interval \(\delta\) seconds, scaling \(h=\sqrt{\alpha\delta}\) yields Brownian variance \(\alpha t\) and covariance \(\alpha\min(t_1,t_2)\); \(\alpha\) is diffusion intensity. The Brownian trace is one continuous but irregular path, not a variance plot. [Random Process p38, Random Process p43, Random Process p45, Random Process p46, Random Process p49, Random Process p50, Random Process p51, Random Process p52, Random Process p53, Random Process p54, Random Process p55, Random Process p56, Random Process p57, Random Process p58]

Stable filtering can create stationary dependence instead of unbounded accumulation. Let \(a_t\) be zero-mean white noise with variance \(\sigma_a^2\) and define \(z_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j a_{t-j}\) where \(\mu\) is level and \(\psi_j\) impulse weights at lag \(j\). Shared shocks give\[\gamma(k)=\sigma_a^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\psi_{j+|k|},\qquad \gamma(0)=\sigma_a^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j^2.\]Here \(\gamma(k)\) is output autocovariance at lag \(k\). An infinite-past square-summable filter has finite stationary variance; absolute summability also guarantees BIBO stability and implies square summability. [System Models p3, System Models p4, System Models p5]Tutorial extension / qualification: These covariance formulas and conditions extend the slides. Failure of absolute summability does not by itself prove nonstationarity, contrary to the blanket “otherwise” statement on System Models p5. White noise need only be uncorrelated; IID shocks are a stronger assumption used by the deck.

AR and MA models encode different sources of memory. With delay operator \(Bz_t=z_{t-1}\), ARMA writes \(\phi(B)(z_t-\mu)=\theta(B)a_t\) where \(\phi,\theta\) are AR and MA polynomials; this deck uses minus signs in both coefficient definitions. AR order counts past outputs, not impulse-response length. Causal stationary AR dynamics require the roots of \(\phi(B)\) outside the unit circle; invertibility instead concerns the MA roots. A first-order Markov process says the present state screens off older states, not that adjacent states are independent. The binary Markov example has stationary probabilities (0.4,0.6) and transition matrix \(P=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/2\\1/3&2/3\end{bmatrix}\); its nontrivial eigenvalue is 1/6. Thus \(R_X(k)=0.36+0.24(1/6)^{|k|}\) and lag-two correlation moment is 0.3667. [System Models p7, System Models p8, System Models p9, System Models p10, System Models p11] [Random Process p73, Random Process p74, Random Process p75, Random Process p76]Tutorial extension / qualification: The root conditions and eigenvalue formula are added derivations. Random Process p76 has the correct final 0.367, but its second printed transition fraction must use denominator 0.4 rather than 0.6.

Study strategy

Compare a random walk with a stable AR(1): both have constant coefficients, but only the stable infinite-past filter has bounded time-independent variance.

Exam pitfall

An AR root in the delay polynomial is reciprocal to its nonzero characteristic mode. Do not swap the two unit-circle tests.

7. State, multivariate prediction, and useful diagnostics

The heated-tank model couples mass and energy balances. Its state-space diagram uses level on one axis and outlet temperature on the other: the curve is a trajectory through possible states, not an output versus time. Linearization evaluates Jacobians at an equilibrium and models deviations from that equilibrium. In \(\dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x+B\mathbf u,\;\mathbf y=C\mathbf x+D\mathbf u\), \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n\) is state, \(\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^m\) input, \(\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^p\) output, and matrix dimensions are \(A:n\times n,\;B:n\times m,\;C:p\times n,\;D:p\times m\). In the given tank, two states, three inputs, and three outputs are distinct counts. A flow increase raises level and outlet flow, while its negative temperature coefficient produces cooling at the specified operating point. [MIMO p4, MIMO p5, MIMO p6, MIMO p7, MIMO p8, MIMO p9, MIMO p10, MIMO p11, MIMO p12, MIMO p14]Tutorial extension / qualification: A linearized deviation model is local; adding the nominal output restores physical units. The displayed explicit differential equations plus algebraic output map do not cover every implicit DAE form.

State evolution exposes the same initial-memory/forcing split as convolution:\[\mathbf x(t)=e^{At}\mathbf x(0)+\int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}B\mathbf u(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D.\]Here \(t\) is elapsed time, \(\tau\) integration time, \(e^{At}\) the matrix exponential, \(s\) the Laplace variable, \(I\) the identity matrix, and \(G\) the transfer matrix for zero initial state. Its entry \(G_{ij}\) maps input \(j\) to output \(i\) with other inputs zero. Matrix exponentials exist even for singular or nondiagonalizable \(A\); the eigenvector formula assumes diagonalizability, and the inverse-based integral formula assumes invertibility. For the two-tank cascade, the second level responds after the first because flow must propagate through the upstream state. [MIMO p15, MIMO p16, MIMO p18, MIMO p19, MIMO p20]Tutorial extension / qualification: The invertibility and diagonalizability qualifications are tutorial refinements.

The overview converts ordered observations into lagged rows and distinguishes recursive from direct multistep forecasting. Its lag-one scatterplots place past value on the horizontal axis and current value on the vertical axis: a diffuse cloud has coefficient near -0.03, while a slanted cluster has coefficient about 0.84. A slowly decaying ACF indicates persistent correlation, not an exact AR order of eight; an AR(1) already has nonzero correlations at every lag. VAR uses lagged vectors and coefficient matrices, allowing cross-variable predictive interactions; state-space introduces a compact hidden memory. External inputs and colored disturbances can be separated as \(Y_t=\nu(B)X_t+\psi(B)a_t\) where \(X_t,Y_t\) are observed input and output, \(\nu\) the input transfer filter, \(\psi\) disturbance filter, and \(a_t\) innovation. Validate residual dependence and forecast horizons, not only fitted error. The airline forecast example reports test RMSE 53.71 versus training RMSE 22.15; the widening long-horizon mismatch argues for examining distribution shift and horizon-specific error. [Time Series Overview p7, Time Series Overview p8, Time Series Overview p9, Time Series Overview p10, Time Series Overview p11, Time Series Overview p12, Time Series Overview p14, Time Series Overview p15, Time Series Overview p16, Time Series Overview p19, Time Series Overview p22, Time Series Overview p23, Time Series Overview p24] [System Models p12, System Models p13, System Models p14, System Models p15, System Models p16]Tutorial extension / qualification: ACF decay does not identify finite memory; the overview p8 wording overstates what its plot proves. ADF tests a unit-root null under a chosen specification, not all forms of nonstationarity. VEC models specifically represent cointegrated integrated series, rather than every multivariate ARIMA. Neural gates mitigate vanishing gradients without universally eliminating them. The 2023 LLM quotation is historical opinion, not a theorem or current capability assessment.

Study strategy

Use dimensions and units to audit every state-space equation, then compare predicted directions of changes with the physical balance equations.

Exam pitfall

A high lag correlation is neither causality nor proof of a specific model order. Test performance must use information available at the forecast origin.

Five synthesis challenges

These worked answers are tutorial additions built from the source examples. Try each before opening the answer.

Why can a stationary input produce a nonstationary output in an LTI system?

A unit-root accumulator has \(h[n]=u[n]\). For independent zero-mean input shocks of variance \(\sigma^2\), initialization at zero gives \(z_n=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\) and \(\operatorname{Var}z_n=n\sigma^2\). Differencing recovers the stationary increments. Constant coefficients concern the operator; stationarity concerns output laws. Foundations: Random Process p52, Random Process p56

Derive a covariance that distinguishes an MA(1) from IID data.

For \(z_t=a_t+0.5a_{t-1}\), zero-mean IID shocks with variance \(\sigma_a^2\) give variance \(1.25\sigma_a^2\), lag-one covariance \(0.5\sigma_a^2\), and zero covariance for larger absolute lags. The overlapping innovation creates dependence despite independent inputs. Foundations: System Models p4, System Models p5

Reconcile the binary Markov example with its printed lag-two calculation.

The stationary distribution is \((0.4,0.6)\). Given state 0, transition to 1 has probability \(0.2/0.4=1/2\); given state 1 it is \(0.4/0.6=2/3\). Thus the lag-two moment is \(0.6[(2/3)^2+(1/3)(1/2)]=11/30\). The printed final 0.367 is right although one intermediate denominator is wrong. Foundations: Random Process p74, Random Process p76

What reaches the output when A/D and D/A use different clocks?

In Sampling Practice p6 the A/D samples at 200 Hz. The 50 Hz cosine maps to \(\pi/2\), while 125 Hz sine maps to \(-3\pi/4\). A D/A at 1,000 Hz therefore reconstructs \(3\cos(2\pi250t)-2\sin(2\pi375t)\) under ideal baseband reconstruction and the stated 500 Hz postfilter. The sign is essential. This is a tutorial worked solution; ideal gain normalization is assumed. Foundations: Sampling Practice p6

Predict the heated-tank steady deviations from a +10 flow step.

Use the rounded MIMO p12 matrices: \(\Delta h_\infty=0.001(10)/0.05=0.2\), \(\Delta T_\infty=-0.0036(10)/0.1=-0.36\), and \(\Delta F_{out,\infty}=50(0.2)=10\). The level time constant is 20 and temperature time constant 10 in the model time units. Add nominal outputs to obtain physical values; the more precise p14 coefficient changes cooling to -0.3585. Foundations: MIMO p12, MIMO p14
